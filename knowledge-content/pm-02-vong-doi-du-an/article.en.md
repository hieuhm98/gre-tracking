# The Project Life Cycle

## 1. Why projects are divided into phases

> **A project without phases is one lump of work whose success or failure you only discover at the last minute.**

**Phasing serves three purposes:**

**1. Creating decision points.** The end of each phase is a **decision gate**: continue, adjust, or stop. **The power to stop a project is the most important one and the least used.**

**2. Limiting damage.** If the project is heading the wrong way, you lose one phase rather than the whole budget.

**3. Clarifying who owns what and when.** Each phase has its own deliverables, so no accountability gaps remain.

**The five phases as PMI divides them:**

| Phase | Central question | Main deliverables |
|-------|------------------|-------------------|
| **Initiation** | *"Should this project exist?"* | Project charter, stakeholder analysis |
| **Planning** | *"How will we do it?"* | Project plan, WBS, risk register, budget |
| **Execution** | *"Build and deliver"* | The actual product |
| **Monitoring & control** | *"Are we on track?"* | Status reports, change requests |
| **Closing** | *"Finish cleanly and what did we learn?"* | Handover records, lessons learned |

> **An important note: monitoring and control is NOT a fourth phase sitting after execution.** It runs **in parallel** with execution from start to finish. Drawing it as a sequential box is the single most common misreading of this model.

---

## 2. Initiation: deciding whether the project should exist

**Initiation exists not to start working but to decide WHETHER to work at all.**

**Four things must be clear before moving on:**

**1. The business problem or opportunity.** Not the solution. *"We need a mobile app"* is not a problem; *"70 percent of customers abandon their cart because they cannot pay on a phone"* is.

**2. Measurable success criteria.** **If nobody can write down what success means, the project will succeed or fail depending on the mood of whoever judges it.**

**3. A sponsor with real authority.** Someone who can approve budget and **resolve conflict between departments**. A project without a real sponsor dies at the first obstacle.

**4. Constraints and assumptions recorded.** The budget ceiling, the immovable deadline, the mandated technology, the person going on maternity leave in June.

**The project charter** summarises all of that, usually in **two to three pages**. Its role is **giving the PM authority to act** and **giving everyone a shared reference when scope is argued about later.**

> **The most expensive mistake in this phase: jumping into detailed planning before knowing the real problem.** You end up with a beautiful plan for solving the wrong thing.

---

## 3. Planning: how detailed is detailed enough

**The output of planning is more than a schedule.** It includes:

- **Scope** and **exclusions**, written explicitly.
- **A WBS** — work broken down far enough to estimate and assign.
- **A schedule** with dependencies and the critical path.
- **A budget** with contingency.
- **An initial risk register.**
- **A communication plan** — who receives what, how often.
- **Acceptance criteria** for each deliverable.

> **The hard question: how detailed should the plan be?**
>
> **The rolling wave principle: detailed for the next two to three months, coarser beyond.** Planning month eleven in detail is waste, because by then every assumption has changed.

**Three common planning mistakes:**

| Mistake | Consequence |
|---------|-------------|
| **No contingency in the plan** | The first risk that materialises makes you late immediately |
| **Assuming everyone works 100 percent on the project** | In reality 60–70 percent is realistic after meetings, support and leave |
| **A plan the PM wrote alone** | People do not commit to estimates they did not make |

**On the people constraint: adding people to a late project usually makes it later**, because newcomers must be trained by exactly the people who are already the bottleneck, and communication channels grow with the square of team size.

---

## 4. Execution and control in parallel

**Execution is where most of the budget is spent.** The PM's job here is **not doing more but keeping the flow of work moving.**

**Four PM jobs during execution:**

- **Clear impediments** before they become delays.
- **Shield the team from noise** — surprise requests, unnecessary meetings, uncontrolled changes.
- **Stop quality being sacrificed quietly** as pressure rises.
- **Update the forecast continuously**, rather than waiting for reporting milestones.

**Control rests on three questions, asked often enough:**

| Question | How to answer honestly |
|----------|------------------------|
| **Where are we against plan?** | Compare work **completed and accepted**, never counting work in progress |
| **Where is the trend heading?** | Three weeks 10 percent behind does not turn into a fast fourth week |
| **What must we decide now?** | Every report should end with a concrete recommendation |

> **The percent-complete trap: work reported 90 percent done for three consecutive weeks is not actually 90 percent done.** The defence: **count only 0 or 100** for each small unit of work, rather than accepting numbers in between.

**On the critical path:** it is the longest chain of work that determines the end date. **A day lost on the critical path is a day lost for the project; a day lost off it may cost nothing.** A PM who does not know their critical path is managing by feel.

## 5. Closing: the most-skipped phase

**Closing is not sending an email saying *"we are done"*.**

**Six things must happen:**

**1. Formal acceptance.** The customer confirms in writing that deliverables meet the agreed criteria.

**2. Handover to operations.** Documentation, access, training, and **who is responsible for incidents once the project team disbands**.

**3. Closing contracts and budget.** Final payments, open purchase orders closed.

**4. Releasing the team.** State each person's last day and where they go next. **Letting a team dissolve with nobody knowing their destination is a reliable way to lose your best people.**

**5. Lessons learned.** Not a list of compliments. **A useful lessons session answers three questions: what worked and should be repeated, what hurt and must change, and what we will do DIFFERENTLY next project.**

**6. Recognising the effort.** It sounds ceremonial, but it decides who wants to join your next project.

> **Why closing gets skipped: once the product is live, everyone has already been pulled onto the next project.** The result is **lessons never recorded and an organisation repeating the same mistake across five consecutive projects.**

**A word on closing FAILED projects too:** a project stopped mid-flight still needs closing properly. **A cancelled project whose lessons were recorded is worth more than a cancelled project buried in silence.**

## 6. Traditional life cycle versus agile

**The five phases do not contradict agile — they are simply performed at a different cadence.**

| | Traditional | Agile |
|---|-------------|-------|
| **Initiation** | Once, at the start | Once for the product, lighter |
| **Planning** | Once, in full detail | **Continuous** — every sprint is a small planning loop |
| **Execution** | One long block | Many short deliverable loops |
| **Control** | Milestone reporting | Visible daily through working software |
| **Closing** | Once, at the end | Every release is a small closing |

> **What does not change between the two models: you still need to know why the project exists, who is accountable, what risks are hanging, and how you will know you are done.** Agile changes the *cadence* of answering those questions, not their necessity.

**How to choose:** clear and stable scope, heavy compliance requirements, a fixed-price contract → **lean traditional**. Uncertain requirements, a need for early user feedback → **lean agile**. **Most real projects sit in between and run a hybrid.**

## 7. Key takeaways

- **An unphased project is one lump whose success you learn at the last minute.**
- Phasing exists to **create decision points, limit damage, and clarify accountability**.
- **The power to stop a project is the most important and least used.**
- **Monitoring and control runs in parallel with execution**, not after it.
- Initiation needs **a business problem, measurable success criteria, a sponsor with authority, and recorded constraints**.
- ***"We need a mobile app"* is not a problem** — it is a solution.
- **Without written success criteria, success depends on the mood of whoever judges.**
- **A charter is two to three pages**, giving the PM authority and everyone a shared reference.
- **Rolling wave planning:** detailed for two to three months, coarser beyond.
- **Assuming 100 percent allocation is wrong** — 60 to 70 percent is realistic.
- **People do not commit to estimates they did not make.**
- **Adding people to a late project usually makes it later.**
- During execution the PM **clears impediments, blocks noise, guards quality, updates forecasts**.
- **Count only work completed and accepted**, never work in progress.
- **The 90 percent trap:** count 0 or 100 for each small unit of work.
- **A day lost on the critical path is a day lost overall** — not knowing it means managing by feel.
- Closing covers **acceptance, handover, contract closure, releasing the team, lessons, recognition**.
- **Letting a team dissolve with no destination reliably loses your best people.**
- **Lessons must answer what we will do DIFFERENTLY**, not list compliments.
- **A cancelled project still needs closing** so its lessons are not buried with it.
- Agile **changes the cadence of the life cycle's questions, not their necessity**.

## 8. Summary

- The five phases exist to **create decision points**, and the most important gate is the power to stop.
- **Initiation decides whether the project should exist**, and rushing into detailed planning is the costliest mistake.
- **Control runs in parallel with execution**, based on accepted work rather than self-reported percentages.
- **Closing is the most-skipped phase**, which is exactly why organisations repeat the same mistakes.
