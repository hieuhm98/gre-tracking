# Domain Knowledge, Certifications & Interviews

## 1. Why domain knowledge drives a BA's value

> **Analysis technique can be learned in months. Domain knowledge takes years — and that is exactly why it determines your value.**

**Domain knowledge is understanding the business field** the system serves: banking, insurance, logistics, healthcare, retail, manufacturing.

**Three things only domain knowledge gives you:**

**1. You ask questions others never think of.** A BA who knows insurance will wonder *"how is the premium calculated on a mid-term renewal?"* — a question a generalist does not know exists.

**2. You notice when something is wrong.** When a customer describes a process that deviates from the industry norm, you know to probe rather than transcribe.

**3. You are trusted faster.** Business people open up to someone who understands their work and close down with someone who needs every term explained.

> **This is also why career changers from accounting, banking, or healthcare have a genuine advantage.** They bring the hardest thing to teach, and what they lack can be learned in months.

---

## 2. How to learn a new domain

When you join a project in an unfamiliar field, this route works:

**Week 1 — Build the vocabulary.**

- Start a **personal glossary** on day one. Record and ask about every term you do not understand.
- **Do not pretend to understand.** *"I am not clear on this term — could you explain it?"* builds more credibility than nodding and guessing wrong.

**Weeks 2–3 — Understand the value flow.**

- Draw the **end-to-end process** of the core business: where money and value travel, who participates at each step.
- Ask the broadest question: ***"How does this business make money, and what makes it lose money?"***

**Week 4 onward — Go deep where needed.**

- Read the **regulations and industry standards** touching your area.
- **Read the real data** — run queries, see which values actually appear, look for exceptions.
- **Read the support ticket repository** — a ready-made list of real pain points.

> **The fastest learning source few people use: sit beside an operations person for half a day and watch them work.** You will learn more than a week of reading process documentation, because you see the gap between the documented and the actual process.

**Three signs you have enough domain knowledge:**

- You **predict the answer** before asking, and are usually right.
- You **notice when someone states the business incorrectly**.
- Business people **ask your opinion** about matters outside the software.

## 3. International BA certifications

**The best-known set comes from the IIBA, based on BABOK:**

| Certification | For | Indicative experience requirement |
|--------------|-----|----------------------------------|
| **ECBA** (Entry Certificate in Business Analysis) | Newcomers or students | No experience required; training hours needed |
| **CCBA** (Certification of Capability in Business Analysis) | BAs with a few years | About 3,750 BA hours in the last 7 years |
| **CBAP** (Certified Business Analysis Professional) | Experienced BAs | About 7,500 BA hours in the last 10 years |

**Other certifications worth knowing:** **PMI-PBA** (from PMI, business analysis within a project management context) and agile credentials such as **CSPO** for the Product Owner role.

### Are certifications worth it?

**The honest answer: it depends, and it matters less than many people think.**

**Certifications help when:**

- You are **changing careers** and need a signal to employers that you are serious.
- A company or client **explicitly requires** them in a tender.
- You want to **systematise knowledge** you acquired piecemeal through experience.

**Certifications help less when:**

- You already have **real experience and a clear portfolio** — interviewers will ask about projects, not certificates.
- You expect them to **substitute for experience** — they do not.

> **A blunt assessment: in most markets, a project you built yourself and can discuss for twenty minutes is worth more than a certificate.** A certificate proves you studied; a portfolio proves you can do the work.

## 4. Preparing for a BA interview

**Employers assess four things, in this order of importance:**

**1. Analytical thinking.** How do you approach an ambiguous problem?

**2. Communication.** Do you explain something complex clearly?

**3. Foundational knowledge.** Do you understand the development process, analysis technique, and enough technology to work with the team?

**4. Experience and domain.** What have you done, and does it fit their field?

> **A key point: newcomers usually prepare items 3 and 4 but get rejected on 1 and 2.** You can memorise BABOK definitions and still fail if you cannot demonstrate how you think.

## 5. Common fresher interview questions

**Group 1 — Motivation and understanding of the role**

| Question | What they actually want to hear |
|----------|--------------------------------|
| *"Why do you want to be a BA?"* | Durable reasons: enjoying understanding problems, enjoying connecting people. **Avoid** saying because you do not want to code or because the pay is good |
| *"What does a BA do?"* | The four groups: elicit, analyse and model, specify, stay to completion |
| *"How does a BA differ from a PO and a PM?"* | A BA asks what the solution must do; a PO asks what to build first and why; a PM asks how to deliver on time |

**Group 2 — Analysis technique**

| Question | The key points to include |
|----------|--------------------------|
| *"How do functional and nonfunctional requirements differ?"* | Functional describes what the system does; nonfunctional describes how WELL it does it |
| *"Write a user story for feature X"* | All three parts, especially the **so that** clause stating value; plus acceptance criteria covering a failure case |
| *"How do you know a requirement is good enough?"* | Verifiable, unambiguous, covers exceptions, and a tester can write test cases from it |
| *"Which elicitation techniques do you use?"* | Name several AND state which technique suits which situation |

**Group 3 — Situational**

| Question | A good approach |
|----------|----------------|
| *"A customer asks for a feature you think is unnecessary. What do you do?"* | Ask why repeatedly to find the real need; if you still differ, present the trade-off and let them decide |
| *"Two departments want contradictory things. How do you handle it?"* | Clarify with examples and models; if it is a priority conflict, take the data to whoever has authority |
| *"A developer says your requirement is not feasible. How do you react?"* | Ask what specifically makes it complex and whether a cheaper option meets the same need |
| *"You find a missed requirement close to the deadline. What do you do?"* | Assess impact, present the trade-off, let the authority decide — never quietly hide it |

> **The principle for situational answers: always show that you CLARIFY and SUPPLY DATA, while the DECISION belongs to whoever has authority.** Candidates who answer *"I would just decide the most sensible option myself"* are usually marked down for not understanding role boundaries.

## 6. Building a portfolio without experience

**The classic problem: employers want experience, but you need a job to get experience.**

**How to break the loop: create work products yourself.**

**The most effective personal project:**

1. **Pick a real application you use daily** — a food delivery app, an e-wallet, a marketplace.
2. **Write the analysis documentation as if you were its BA**:
   - Identify user classes and stakeholders.
   - Chart the main business process with a flowchart or BPMN.
   - Write 5–10 user stories with full acceptance criteria including failure cases.
   - List the business rules you can infer from the app's behaviour.
   - Wireframe one feature you would improve, with your reasoning.
   - Write a few test cases from the acceptance criteria.
3. **Pick one real problem** in that application and propose a solution with a trade-off analysis.

> **The biggest differentiator in a fresher interview is not a certificate but something you built yourself and can discuss for twenty minutes.** It proves you **can do the work**, not merely that you **studied the theory**.

**Three other experience sources for beginners:**

- **An internal project at your current job** — if you work in accounting or operations, volunteer for your department's digitisation project.
- **An internship or volunteer project** for a nonprofit.
- **Playing the BA role in a study group** — build a small product with a few people and take the analysis role.

## 7. Key takeaways

- **Analysis technique takes months; domain knowledge takes years** — and it is what determines your value.
- Domain knowledge gives you three things: **questions others never think of, noticing when something is wrong, and faster trust**.
- **Career changers have a genuine advantage** because they bring the hardest thing to teach.
- Entering a new domain: **start a personal glossary on day one and never pretend to understand**.
- The strongest broad question: ***"How does this business make money, and what makes it lose money?"***
- **Half a day beside an operations person teaches more than a week of reading process documentation.**
- Three signs of sufficient domain knowledge: **predicting answers correctly, noticing errors, being asked for opinions beyond the software**.
- The three IIBA certifications: **ECBA for newcomers, CCBA for a few years, CBAP for experienced BAs**.
- **Certifications help when changing careers or when a client requires them**, less so when you already have a portfolio.
- **A certificate proves you studied; a portfolio proves you can do the work.**
- Employers assess in this order: **analytical thinking, communication, foundational knowledge, experience and domain**.
- **Newcomers prepare knowledge but get rejected on thinking and communication.**
- For *"why do you want to be a BA"*, **avoid saying because you do not want to code or because of the pay**.
- The rule for situational answers: **you CLARIFY and SUPPLY DATA, the DECISION belongs to the authority**.
- Candidates who say *"I would just decide myself"* are marked down for **not understanding role boundaries**.
- **A personal project breaks the need-experience-to-get-experience loop.**
- **Something you built and can discuss for twenty minutes is worth more than a certificate.**

## 8. Summary

- **Domain knowledge is the hardest thing to teach and raises your value fastest** — learn it methodically rather than by osmosis.
- **Certifications are a signal, not a capability** — most useful when changing careers or when a client requires one.
- BA interviews assess **how you think first, what you know second**.
- **A self-created portfolio is the most practical route** to demonstrating capability before you have real project experience.
