# Who Is the Project Manager?

## 1. Definition and core responsibilities

> **A Project Manager is accountable for getting a project to its destination within committed constraints — not for building the product.**

**A PM does not write code, design screens, or write detailed requirements.** The PM's job is **creating the conditions in which the people doing those things can do them well**, and **keeping the whole project visible to decision-makers.**

**Six responsibilities that cannot be handed to anyone else:**

| Responsibility | What it means in practice |
|----------------|---------------------------|
| **Scope** | Knowing what the project includes and **especially what it excludes** |
| **Schedule** | Having a plan, and knowing how far off it you are |
| **Resources** | Enough people, with the right skills, at the right time |
| **Risk** | Spotting early with a response ready, rather than reacting to fires |
| **Communication** | The right information to the right people at the right time |
| **Quality** | Results meeting the agreed standard, not merely arriving on time |

> **If you must define the PM job in one sentence: a PM turns uncertainty into decisions that can be acted on.**

**A very common misreading: that a PM tracks progress and updates spreadsheets.** That is a *project administrator*. **A real PM makes trade-off decisions**, and every one of them displeases somebody.

---

## 2. The constraint triangle and the truth about it

**The classic three constraints: scope, time, cost — with quality in the middle.**

> **The rule: you cannot fix all three. Fix all three and quality becomes the only remaining variable, and it will drop without anyone deciding to let it.**

**Three ways to respond under pressure:**

| When pressed | The right response |
|--------------|--------------------|
| *"It must finish a month earlier"* | *"Fine — which items do we drop, or how many people do we add, and I need two weeks' notice"* |
| *"Cost cannot increase"* | *"Then scope or time has to give"* |
| *"All three stay fixed"* | **This is where you say no, or record the risk in writing** |

**What PMs commonly get wrong:** accepting the commitment and hoping the team will work overtime to cover it. **Overtime borrows a few weeks and repays them with months: more defects, good people leaving, and a sustained drop in productivity.**

> **In Agile the triangle is usually inverted: time and cost are fixed (one sprint, one team) while scope is the variable.** This is exactly why the backlog must be ordered — so that when scope must be cut, you cut from the bottom.

## 3. Distinguishing PM, Product Owner, and Product Manager

**These are three different roles, and the most commonly confused ones in the industry.**

| | Project Manager | Product Owner | Product Manager |
|---|-----------------|---------------|-----------------|
| **Central question** | *"How do I get this to the finish line?"* | *"What should the team do next?"* | *"Where should the product go?"* |
| **Time horizon** | Initiation to closing | This sprint and the next few | Many quarters, years |
| **End point** | **A project has an end date** | Continuous | Continuous |
| **Optimises for** | On time, on budget, on scope | Value delivered each sprint | The product's market position |
| **Failure looks like** | Late, over budget, missing scope | The team builds low-value things | Technically correct but wrong market |

> **The core difference: a PROJECT has an end date, a PRODUCT does not.** So a PM optimises for finishing, while POs and Product Managers optimise for continuing better.

**In Vietnamese practice:** in an outsourcing company the PM is the central role because every contract is a project with an end date. **In a product company the PM role is usually thinner** and most of the responsibility is split between the Product Owner and the Engineering Manager. **The same PM title at these two kinds of company can be two completely different jobs** — ask explicitly in interviews.

## 4. Four skills that decide success

**1. Deciding with incomplete information.**

This is what separates a good PM from an average one. **You will never have enough data.** Your job is **deciding with what you have, recording the assumption, and adjusting as you learn** — rather than waiting for certainty that never arrives.

> **A late decision is usually worse than an imperfect one made early and corrected in time.**

**2. Layered communication.**

The same situation must be told three different ways:

- **To the team:** detailed, specific, focused on what to do.
- **To management:** impact on schedule, cost, risk — and **what you recommend**.
- **To the customer:** effect on their commitments, and the options.

**This is not saying different things than the truth, it is choosing the right level of detail.** A PM who reads a full technical report to the board has not done their job.

**3. Influence without authority.**

**A PM is usually nobody's line manager.** You do not set salaries or promotions. **Your tools are credibility, clarity, and whether you actually shield the team.** A PM who pushes all pressure down loses their influence after a single project.

**4. Saying no with evidence.**

**Refusing without data is obstruction; refusing with an impact analysis is protecting the project.** *"It cannot be done"* is a poor sentence. *"It can, but it pushes UAT to the 15th and needs another tester — here are two options"* is a PM's sentence.

## 5. A PM's day and week

**The typical shape of the work — useful for knowing what the job really looks like:**

**Daily:**

- **Look at the real state** of work in flight, rather than waiting for Friday.
- **Clear impediments** the team raises — the highest-value work of the day.
- **Answer blocking questions**, or find someone who can.

**Weekly:**

- **Compare actual against plan** and update the forecast, not just the record of the past.
- **Review the risk register** — add new ones, close spent ones, update changed ones.
- **Report status** to stakeholders, including what you need from them.
- **Talk privately with a few team members** to hear what never surfaces in meetings.

> **A sign a PM is doing the wrong job: a whole week spent updating ticket statuses.** That is work tools should do. **A PM's time belongs to clearing impediments and making trade-off decisions.**

## 6. Career paths and maturity signals

**Common routes into the role:**

- **From engineering:** a strong developer or tester → team lead → PM. **Advantage: understands the real work and estimates credibly. Risk: tends to jump in and do it rather than letting the team.**
- **From analysis:** BA → PM. **Advantage: strong grasp of the business and stakeholders. Risk: weaker on technical estimation and technology risk.**
- **From coordination:** PMO or project coordinator → PM. **Advantage: strong on process and documentation. Risk: leaning toward form over substance.**

**Three signs of a mature PM:**

**1. Bad news arrives early.** The team raises problems instead of hiding them until they cannot be hidden. **This only happens when the PM does not punish the messenger.**

**2. Forecasts get steadily more accurate.** Not because the team got faster, but because the PM learned how their team actually works.

**3. The project runs while the PM takes a week off.** If everything stops, the PM is a bottleneck rather than an enabler.

> **On certifications:** PMP and PRINCE2 help in traditional environments and for passing CV screens; PMI-ACP and Scrum certifications suit agile ones. **But no certification substitutes for one project you took to the finish and can narrate in detail.**

## 7. Key takeaways

- **A PM is accountable for getting the project to its destination, not for building the product.**
- Six responsibilities: **scope, schedule, resources, risk, communication, quality**.
- **A PM turns uncertainty into decisions that can be acted on.**
- **A PM who only tracks progress and updates spreadsheets is a project administrator.**
- **You cannot fix all three constraints** — doing so lets quality drop without anyone deciding.
- **Overtime borrows a few weeks and repays with months** of defects, attrition and lost productivity.
- **In Agile time and cost are fixed and scope is the variable** — hence the ordered backlog.
- **A project has an end date, a product does not** — the core PM versus PO difference.
- **The same PM title in outsourcing and in product companies is two different jobs.**
- **A late decision is usually worse than an imperfect one made early and corrected.**
- Layered communication: **the team needs detail, management needs impact and a recommendation, customers need effect and options**.
- **A PM usually has no formal authority** — the tools are credibility, clarity, and shielding the team.
- **A PM who pushes all pressure down loses influence after one project.**
- **Refusing without data is obstruction; refusing with impact analysis protects the project.**
- The highest-value daily work is **clearing the team's impediments**.
- **Update the forecast, not just the record of the past.**
- **A PM spending the week on ticket statuses is doing work tools should do.**
- **Bad news arrives early only when the PM does not punish the messenger.**
- **A project that stops when the PM is away means the PM is the bottleneck.**
- **No certification substitutes for one project you took to the finish and can narrate.**

## 8. Summary

- The PM job is **making trade-off decisions and enabling others to work well**, not tracking spreadsheets.
- **The constraint triangle is a daily negotiation tool**, and Agile inverts it by fixing time and cost.
- **PM, PO and Product Manager differ in time horizon and in whether the work has an end point.**
- **The four decisive skills are deciding under uncertainty, layered communication, influence without authority, and saying no with evidence.**
