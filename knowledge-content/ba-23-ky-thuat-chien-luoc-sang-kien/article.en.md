# Strategy & Initiative Horizon Techniques

The top two horizons have their own set of techniques, and they get confused far more often
than the delivery-horizon ones — because the source material describes them in corporate
language that is almost interchangeable. "Optimise the allocation of resources", "support
decision-making and prioritisation", "improve collaboration among teams" appear in the
definitions of several different techniques.

The only way to separate them is to learn each technique's **distinctive phrase**.

## Strategy horizon techniques — the definition table

| Technique | What it is used for at the strategy horizon |
|---|---|
| **Purpose alignment model** | Prioritise potential initiatives and understand the **optimum resourcing mix** and the overall focus of initiatives across the organisation |
| **Portfolio Kanban** | Provide **real-time visibility of the progress** of initiatives across the portfolio; often used with balanced scorecards and value stream maps to optimise resource allocation |
| **Product roadmap** | Communicate the **expected future direction of the product** and improve collaboration among teams in different initiatives; also supports decision-making and prioritisation |
| **Value stream mapping** | Understand the **creation of value across the whole customer experience**, in order to prioritise, plan and integrate value creation and waste reduction between initiatives |
| **Real options** | Understand the **appropriate timeframe in which to make a decision** — that is, how long a decision can responsibly be deferred |
| **Planning workshop** | Plan the allocation of resources across multiple initiatives and give a shared understanding of the purpose of a new initiative |
| **Minimal Viable Product** | Prioritise the allocation of resources and **increase the speed of organisational learning** |
| **Value modeling** | Inform research and development, marketing and sales, looking at the customers of the organisation as a whole |

## The four most confusable techniques

If you remember only four phrases, remember these — they are what separate the four
techniques that get swapped most often:

- Purpose alignment model → **optimum resourcing mix** across initiatives
- Portfolio Kanban → **real-time progress** across the portfolio
- Product roadmap → **expected future direction of the product**
- Value stream mapping → **the whole customer experience**

And a fifth, for the one technique that answers a question about *timing*:

- Real options → **how long this decision can wait**

Real options is the only technique in the table that is about **when**, not about **what**.
If the question asks about the timing of a decision, you need not read the other three
options.

## Vision statements and impact metrics

A **vision statement** in an agile context is **a concise statement of goals, scope and
expected benefits** — few words, usually with a visual. Two familiar distractors are
rejected outright:

- *"A formal document with robust descriptions of goals, scope and expected benefits"* — a
  dense formal document is not an agile way of working.
- *"A project charter document to initiate the project"* — it sounds logical, but a charter
  is **left over from older ways of working**; an entire charter is not needed here.

An **impact metric** in a visioning exercise must be **customer- and value-focused**, and
must have a **baseline and a goal value**. Compare:

| Example | A good impact metric? |
|---|---|
| Increase the number of purchases per customer | ✔ Yes — describes a change in customer behaviour |
| Reduce the time it takes to complete an application | ✔ Yes — describes a change in customer experience |
| Create loyalty and repeat visitors | ✔ Yes — describes a change in customer behaviour |
| Implement an upgrade to the software | ✘ No — this is an **output the team will produce**, not an impact on a customer |

The tidy rule: **a metric that describes what the team will BUILD is never an impact
metric.** An impact metric describes a change in customer or business behaviour.

## Kano analysis and impact mapping

**Kano analysis** looks at which product characteristics will act as a **differentiator**
and **drive customer satisfaction**, so that effort is not wasted on features that do not
move satisfaction. If a question carries all three cues — *differentiator* + *customer
satisfaction* + *avoiding waste on features* — the answer is Kano, not impact mapping, not
value modeling, not portfolio Kanban.

An **impact map** is a lightweight visual that breaks organisational goals down into
specific deliverables, keeping stakeholders focused on the **WHY** rather than just the
features. Its four elements, in order:

> **Goal → Actors → Impacts → Deliverables**

The familiar distractors are real agile nouns in the wrong combination: *"epics, themes,
features and stories"*, *"impacts, components, features and stories"*, *"strategic plans,
releases, sprints and stories"*. All real vocabulary, none of them impact map elements.

The same family contains one **wholly invented** name: *"business value map"* — not a
technique in the Agile Extension to the BABOK at all. Eliminate on sight.

## Relative estimating

**Relative estimating** is a **collaborative technique** in which dialogue about knowledge,
historical experience and assumptions is critical. Practically, it uses **order of
magnitude** and **comparison of an item to previously delivered items** to judge how long
the item will likely take.

Four misreadings are explicitly rejected:

- It is **not a formula** — no calculation produces the number.
- It is **not a tool** that generates a Fibonacci number from a user story.
- It is **not something the BA and Scrum Master do** on the team's behalf for leadership.
- It is **not something the BA and Product Owner do** for the team. The **team** estimates.

Historical data *may* be considered in a relative estimate, but it is not the main
component. And "the team's best guess based on experience" is too thin — it misses both the
comparison and the order-of-magnitude aspects.

## Backlog refinement and measuring a value stream

**Backlog refinement at the initiative horizon** is prioritising and sequencing features,
and it happens **ongoing and multiple times throughout the initiative** — not once at
project initiation, not inside the iteration planning workshop, and not in a strategy
workshop before the initiative starts. Backlog refinement is also the **key input** to a
planning workshop.

**How do you know an implemented increment of a new value stream map is effective?** By
**measuring how each changed piece is improving the value stream overall**. Three wrong
measures:

- Counting the number of features delivered — that measures **output**, not value.
- Reporting the milestones met — also an output measure.
- Asking customers what they think — customer opinion matters, but only alongside measures
  aligned to value, never on its own.

## Key takeaways

- Learn the **distinctive phrase**: purpose alignment = *resourcing mix*; portfolio Kanban =
  *real-time across the portfolio*; product roadmap = *expected future direction*; value
  stream mapping = *whole customer experience*; real options = *how long a decision can wait*.
- A vision statement is **concise** on goals, scope, benefits. A project charter is left over
  from older ways of working.
- An impact metric must be a **change in customer behaviour**, with a baseline and a goal.
  What the team will build is never an impact metric.
- Kano = **differentiator + satisfaction**. Impact map = **goal → actors → impacts →
  deliverables**. "Business value map" is an invented name.
- Relative estimating = **collaborative + order of magnitude + comparison to delivered
  items**, done by the **team**.
- Initiative-horizon backlog refinement is **continuous**, and is the input to a planning
  workshop.
