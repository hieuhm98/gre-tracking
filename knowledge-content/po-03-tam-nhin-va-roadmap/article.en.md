# Product Vision & Roadmap

## 1. Why vision matters more than you think

> **Without a clear product vision, every prioritization decision becomes arbitrary.**

When two items both look reasonable, what helps you choose is not who asked more loudly but **which one brings the product closer to where it needs to go**. That is exactly what a vision does.

**Three signs a team lacks a clear vision:**

- Every priority argument ends with *"management wants it"*.
- The backlog contains many features unrelated to each other.
- Asked *"why are we building this?"*, the common answer is *"because customer X asked"* rather than a strategic reason.

---

## 2. Writing a good product vision

**A simple, effective template:**

> **For** *(target user group)*
> **who** *(have which need or pain)*
> **our product is** *(what kind of product)*
> **that** *(key benefit)*
> **unlike** *(the current alternative)*
> **our product** *(the core differentiator)*

**An example:**

> **For** chemists and stockroom staff in manufacturing companies,
> **who** lose hours each week finding chemicals and tracking order status,
> **the Chemical Tracking System is** a centralised chemical management system
> **that** lets them find, request, and track chemicals in minutes rather than days,
> **unlike** the current approach of scattered emails and spreadsheets,
> **our system** shows exactly which chemicals are in stock and who currently holds them.

**Four criteria for a good vision:**

- **Specific about the audience** — not *"all users"*.
- **Names a real pain**, not a generic wish.
- **Has a differentiator** against the current alternative, even when that alternative is doing it by hand.
- **Short enough for the team to remember** — if you must open a document to read it, it is not doing its job.

> **An effective test: ask three people on the team to describe the product vision in their own words.** If the three answers differ substantially, the vision has not been communicated successfully — however well it was written.

## 3. Measurable product goals

**A vision gives direction; goals tell you how far you have come.**

**Turning a vision into measurable goals:**

| The vision says | The measurable goal |
|-----------------|--------------------|
| *"Find chemicals in minutes rather than days"* | Reduce mean time from request to receipt from 3 days to under 4 hours, during Q3 |
| *"Reduce manual effort"* | Cut emails about chemical orders by 50 percent within 6 months |
| *"Improve stock accuracy"* | Discrepancy between system and physical stock below 2 percent |

> **The principle: goals must measure a business OUTCOME, not the team's output.** *"Ship 20 features this quarter"* is not a product goal — it is an output metric, and it says nothing about the value created.

## 4. Outcome-based roadmaps, not feature-based

This is the most important difference between a useful roadmap and a harmful one.

| | **Feature roadmap** | **Outcome roadmap** |
|---|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Contents** | A list of features with release dates | Business outcomes to achieve within a time band |
| **Example** | *"Q2: build the stock dashboard"* | *"Q2: reduce chemical search time below 4 hours"* |
| **When you learn something new** | You must keep the promise about that specific feature | You are free to change how the outcome is achieved |
| **Risk** | Becomes a hard commitment, losing flexibility | Requires more organizational maturity to accept |

**Why feature roadmaps do harm:**

- They **become commitments** in stakeholders' eyes, however clearly you label them intentions.
- They **lock in solutions** before you learn anything new from users.
- They **encourage measuring the wrong thing** — the team is judged on delivering features rather than creating value.

> **A presentation style that preserves flexibility: three horizons.**
>
> - **Now** — specific, committed, detailed.
> - **Next** — direction is clear but the detail is not settled.
> - **Later** — themes and hypotheses only, nothing committed.
>
> This conveys **direction** without creating **false commitments** about distant things.

---

## 5. Communicating the roadmap to stakeholders

**The classic problem: a stakeholder hears "Q3" and understands "definitely there in Q3".**

**Four ways to reduce misunderstanding:**

**1. State the confidence level.** *"The Now items are committed; Next is the direction we are heading; Later are hypotheses to test."*

**2. Present the reasoning, not just the content.** When stakeholders understand **why** something is prioritized, they argue about goals rather than jostling for list positions.

**3. Update regularly and state what changed.** A stale roadmap loses trust faster than one that changes often.

**4. Tie every item to a measurable goal.** This shifts the conversation from *"where is my feature"* to *"which outcome are we pursuing"*.

> **A key principle: a roadmap is a tool for COMMUNICATING DIRECTION, not a delivery contract.** If your organization treats it as a contract, adjust the presentation — fewer specific dates, more explicit confidence levels.

## 6. From vision down to backlog

**The full chain makes every item traceable to its reason for existing:**

**Vision** → **Product goals** (measurable) → **Roadmap themes** → **Epics** → **Stories** → **Acceptance criteria**

**The check question at each level:**

| Level | Question |
|-------|----------|
| Goal | Is it measurable, and who is accountable for that number? |
| Roadmap theme | Which goal does it serve? |
| Epic | If completed, how much closer does the goal get? |
| Story | Which epic does it belong to, and what value does the *so that* clause state? |
| AC | How do we know this story is done right, including failure cases? |

> **A very effective backlog health check: pick five items at random from the top of the backlog and ask which product goal each serves.**
>
> **If an item traces to no goal**, either the goals are incomplete or that item should not be at the top. **Both are valuable findings.**

## 7. Key takeaways

- **Without a clear vision, every prioritization decision becomes arbitrary.**
- Three signs of a missing vision: **arguments ending with management wants it, a disconnected backlog, the common reason being customer X asked**.
- A good vision is **specific about the audience, names a real pain, has a differentiator, and is short enough to remember**.
- **If you must open a document to read it, the vision is not doing its job.**
- The test: **ask three team members to describe the vision in their own words** and see if they match.
- **Goals must measure business outcomes, not the team's output.**
- ***"Ship 20 features this quarter"* is not a product goal** but an output metric.
- **Feature roadmaps become commitments** in stakeholders' eyes however you label them.
- Feature roadmaps **lock in solutions before you learn anything new** from users.
- **The Now / Next / Later horizons** convey direction without false commitments about distant items.
- Communicating a roadmap: **state confidence, present reasoning, update regularly, tie to measurable goals**.
- **A stale roadmap loses trust faster than one that changes often.**
- **A roadmap communicates direction; it is not a delivery contract.**
- The chain: **vision → goals → roadmap themes → epics → stories → acceptance criteria**.
- The backlog check: **pick five top items and ask which goal each serves**.
- **An item tracing to no goal** means either the goals are incomplete or it does not belong at the top.

## 8. Summary

- **The vision says why the product exists; goals say how far you have come; the roadmap says which way you are heading.**
- **Outcome roadmaps preserve the flexibility** that feature roadmaps give away.
- **Now / Next / Later** balances clarity against premature commitment.
- Every item at the top of the backlog should **trace to a specific product goal**.
