# The Customer Perspective & Requirements Agreement

## 1. The expectation gap

Picture a familiar conversation. A senior manager says: *"We need a chemical tracking system. Can you build it in five months?"* The IT manager replies: *"Before I can commit to a schedule, we need to understand the requirements."* The manager is puzzled: *"I just told you my requirements."*

He did not state requirements. He stated a **business objective**. That is not enough to know what software to build, or how long it will take.

Without adequate customer involvement, the inescapable outcome is an **expectation gap** — a gulf between what customers really need and what developers deliver based on what they heard at the beginning of the project.

The only way to narrow that gap is **frequent contact points** with suitable customer representatives:

- Interviews and conversations.
- Requirements reviews.
- User interface design walkthroughs.
- Prototype evaluations.
- With agile: user feedback on each small increment of executable software.

Each contact point is a chance to **pull the two lines back together**. Immediately after each contact the gap starts widening again as development proceeds — so the more frequent the contact points, the closer the delivered product tracks the real need.

> A true story: a development team built a new internal information system having obtained negligible user input. On unveiling day the users rejected it as completely unacceptable. The team had to fix it, and the next project in the queue had to wait. Developers were chagrined, users were unhappy, executives were upset about wasted money and the opportunity cost. Companies **always** fix the system when they get the requirements wrong — and it always costs far more than engaging user representatives from the outset.

## 2. Who is the customer?

A **stakeholder** is a person, group, or organization that is **actively involved** in a project, is **affected** by its process or outcome, or **can influence** its process or outcome.

The containment relationship matters:

**Stakeholders ⊃ Customers ⊃ Users**

- A **customer** is an individual or organization that derives either **direct or indirect benefit** from a product. Customers may request, pay for, select, specify, use, or receive the output generated by the product.
- **Users** (end users) are a subset of customers — the people who actually **operate** the product.
  - **Direct users** operate the product hands-on.
  - **Indirect users** receive outputs without touching the system, such as a warehouse manager who gets an automatic daily activity report by email.

Not every stakeholder is a customer: legal staff, compliance auditors, suppliers, contractors, and venture capitalists are all stakeholders but do not benefit directly from the product.

**Who supplies which level:**

| Source | Supplies |
|--------|----------|
| Funding sponsor / senior management | Business requirements |
| The people who will actually use the product | User requirements |
| Marketing / product manager (when users are unreachable) | Customer surrogate for user requirements |

> **Trap:** Senior customers often believe they can speak for the actual users. They are usually too far removed from the daily work to do so accurately. If there is a serious disconnect between the acquiring customer who pays and the end users, major problems are guaranteed.

### The case of the missing stakeholder

One project was nearly finished with elicitation when the BA asked: *"Are you sure we have the tax calculation steps right in this flow?"* The answer: *"Oh, I do not know. I do not own tax. That is the tax department."* For months nobody had spoken to the tax department — the team did not even know it existed. When they finally met, a long list of missed requirements around the legal implications appeared, and the project was delayed several months.

**The lesson:** use an **organization chart** to search for every department the new system will affect.

## 3. The customer-developer partnership

An excellent product results from a well-executed design based on excellent requirements. Excellent requirements result from **effective collaboration** between developers and customers — a **partnership**, not a one-way handover.

Collaboration only works when everyone knows **what they need to be successful** and also **understands and respects** what their collaborators need to be successful. As project pressures rise it is easy to forget that all stakeholders share one objective: build a product that provides adequate business value and rewards to everyone involved.

**The business analyst is typically the point person who has to forge this partnership.**

To make that concrete rather than a slogan, Wiegers proposes two symmetrical lists: a **Bill of Rights** (10 customer rights) and a **Bill of Responsibilities** (10 customer responsibilities). The flip side of a right is always a responsibility.

> Early in the project, have the key stakeholders review both lists and **negotiate to reach a meeting of the minds**. Write down how you agree to work together. This reduces friction later, when one party expects something the other is not willing or able to provide.

---

## 4. The Requirements Bill of Rights — 10 customer rights

Customers have the right to:

1. **Expect BAs to speak your language.** Requirements discussions should centre on your business needs and tasks, using business vocabulary. You should not have to wade through technical jargon.
2. **Expect BAs to learn about your business and your objectives.** Invite BAs and developers to observe what you do on the job; if a new system replaces an old one, have them use the current system the way you use it.
3. **Expect BAs to record requirements in an appropriate form.** The BA sorts what stakeholders provide into user requirements, business rules, functional requirements, quality goals, and stores them in a form you find easy to understand.
4. **Receive explanations of requirements practices and deliverables.** If the BA draws a diagram, they should explain its purpose, what the symbols mean, and how to examine it for errors.
5. **Change your requirements.** It is not realistic to expect you to think of everything up front. But **change always has a price** — sometimes a trade-off against other functions, or against schedule and budget.
6. **Expect an environment of mutual respect.** Everyone is on the same side here.
7. **Hear ideas and alternatives for your requirements and their solution.** A good BA suggests improvements to your business processes and proposes capabilities you had not envisioned — rather than *"paving the cow paths"*.
8. **Describe characteristics that will make the product easy to use.** Do not just say *user-friendly* or *robust*; point to what in your current applications feels easy to use and what does not.
9. **Hear about ways to adjust requirements to accelerate development through reuse.** The BA may know of an existing component that comes close to your need; a small adjustment can save considerable time and money.
10. **Receive a system that meets your functional needs and quality expectations.** This is the ultimate right — but it only happens if you clearly state **all your assumptions and expectations**.

---

## 5. The Requirements Bill of Responsibilities — 10 customer responsibilities

Customers have the responsibility to:

1. **Educate BAs and developers about your business.** The intent is not to turn BAs into business experts, but to help them understand your problems and objectives.
2. **Dedicate the time it takes to provide and clarify requirements.** Several focused hours beat the same total time scattered in fragments over weeks.
3. **Be specific and precise when providing input.** Leaving requirements vague means someone must resolve the ambiguity later — and you are the best person to do it, rather than leaving developers to guess.
4. **Make timely decisions when asked.** Developers often cannot proceed with confidence until you render your decision.
5. **Respect a developer assessment of cost and feasibility.** Some requirements are technically infeasible or surprisingly expensive. Sometimes rewriting the requirement makes it attainable — *"instantaneously"* becomes *"within 50 milliseconds"*.
6. **Set realistic priorities in collaboration with developers.** Declaring **every** requirement high priority is neither realistic nor collaborative.
7. **Review requirements and evaluate prototypes.** Requirements should reach you in manageable chunks throughout elicitation, not as a massive tome dumped on your desk once they are *"done"*.
8. **Establish acceptance criteria.** You define the conditions the product must satisfy to be judged acceptable. Testers can judge whether a requirement was implemented correctly, but not always what outcome **you** will consider acceptable.
9. **Promptly communicate changes to the requirements.** The later a change arrives, the greater its impact. Follow the change control process so nothing is lost and every impact is analysed.
10. **Respect the requirements development process.** Feel free to ask why the BA needs certain information — but trust that there is a rationale behind their approach.

## 6. Creating a culture that respects requirements

You will meet resistance. Some people regard requirements work as bureaucratic overhead; others suffered through a large, incomplete, ultimately ignored requirements specification.

Understanding **why** people resist is the first step to addressing it:

- They have **never been exposed** to solid requirements practices.
- They suffered from a **poor implementation** of requirements practices.
- They **do not recognise the price** already paid for working in a casual, unstructured way — because rework is buried in daily project activity, nobody names it as waste.

Effective strategies:

- Point to the **specific pain** the organization has experienced from requirements problems, expressed in units that matter to the audience: money, time, customer dissatisfaction, lost business opportunities.
- Have **developers review requirements as they are evolving**. They see what is coming, spot areas needing clarity, suggest easier ways to do things, and catch missing requirements such as exception handling.
- Engage **QA and testers early**. They have sharp eyes and will find ambiguities and conflicts while building their test cases.
- Remind leadership that business analysis capability must be treated as a **strategic core competency**, or improvements will evaporate once the project ends or after a reorganization.

## 7. Identifying decision makers

A software project involves hundreds of decisions, and many sit on the critical path. Early on, determine **who the requirements decision makers are** and **how they will decide**.

There is usually one primary decision maker, often the key sponsor. A small group representing management, customers, business analysis, development, and marketing generally works best.

That group needs to select a **decision rule**:

- The decision leader chooses, with or without discussion.
- The group votes and the majority rules.
- The group votes, but the result must be **unanimous**.
- The group negotiates to reach **consensus** — everyone can live with the decision and commits to supporting it.
- The leader **delegates** the decision to one individual.
- The group decides, but someone holds **veto authority**.

No single decision rule is globally correct. Set guidelines so you know when to vote, when to seek consensus, and when to delegate — and **choose before you confront the first significant decision**.

## 8. Reaching agreement: sign-off and the requirements baseline

Agreeing on the requirements is at the core of the partnership. Several parties are involved:

- **Customers** agree the requirements address their needs.
- **Developers** agree they understand the requirements and that they are feasible.
- **Testers** agree the requirements are **verifiable**.
- **Management** agrees the requirements will achieve the business objectives.

Many organizations use a **sign-off** ritual. It becomes dangerous when the parties understand it differently:

- A customer signs to unblock coding — then is surprised by the delivered product: *"Sure, I signed off, but I did not have time to read them all. I trusted you guys!"*
- A development manager treats sign-off as a way to **freeze** requirements: *"But you signed off on these, so that is what we are building."*

Both attitudes ignore the reality that it is impossible to know all the requirements early and that requirements will undoubtedly change.

**More important than the ritual is the concept of a requirements baseline** — a snapshot of a reviewed, agreed set of requirements that serves as the basis for further development. The subtext of a signature should be:

> *I agree that this set of requirements represents our best understanding of the requirements for the next portion of this project, and that the solution described will meet our needs as we understand them today. I agree to make future changes in this baseline through the defined change process. I realize that changes might require us to renegotiate cost, resource, and schedule commitments.*

Many organizations print exactly that text on the signature page.

> **Important:** Do not use sign-off as a **weapon**. Treat it as a milestone, with a clear, shared understanding of the activities that lead to it and its implications for future changes.

## 9. What if you do not reach agreement?

Sometimes you cannot obtain sign-off from every relevant stakeholder: logistics, busy schedules, or people reluctant to commit and be held accountable later. If stakeholders fear they will not be able to make changes after approving, they drag their feet — and the project falls into the dreaded trap of **analysis paralysis**.

Many teams send an email saying *"if you do not reply by Friday, I will assume you agree"*. That is one option, but it really equates to **not reaching agreement**, and it risks straining the relationship with the very people whose tacit approval you just assumed.

A better approach:

- Understand **why** they did not feel comfortable signing off and address that directly.
- Move forward **cautiously**, on the assumption that you do **not** have approval from those stakeholders.
- Document the missing approval in your **risk list**, with the likely impact of some requirements being missing or wrong.
- Follow up as part of risk management, and mention positively that the project is moving ahead with the current baseline and that a process exists for them to request changes.

## 10. Agreeing on requirements on agile projects

Agile projects do **not** include a formal sign-off action. Requirements live as **user stories in a product backlog**. The product owner and the team agree in a planning session which stories go into the next iteration, based on **priority** and the team **velocity**. Once chosen, the stories in that iteration are frozen; requested changes are considered for future iterations.

Agile makes no attempt to gain approval for the **full** project scope up front — however the **vision and other business requirements still need to be established at the outset**.

One client asked for sign-off even while following agile. The team handled it creatively: they asked users to sign that, **at that moment in time**, there were no major requirements missing that they knew about and no major issues with what had been written down. This kept the agile spirit intact: nobody is forced into a corner over a massive document they barely understand, and users keep the right to realise later that they need something new.

As consultant Nanette Brown put it:

> Even in an agile environment the concept of sign-off can fill a valid function. Agile tells us to *embrace change*, but the concept of change only exists with respect to a **reference point**. If you position a sign-off as a lightweight ceremony acknowledging that *we are here*, I think it is fine.

On agile projects the **ultimate sign-off** is the product owner accepting the working, tested software delivered from the iteration.

## Key takeaways

- The **expectation gap** is the gulf between real customer need and what gets delivered. Close it with **frequent contact points**, not one round of elicitation.
- Containment: **stakeholders ⊃ customers ⊃ users**. Not every stakeholder is a customer.
- **Business requirements** come from the sponsor; **user requirements** must come from people who will actually use the product.
- The **Bill of Rights** and **Bill of Responsibilities** are two faces of one partnership — negotiate and agree on them at project start.
- Right 5 (changing requirements) always comes with the reality that **change has a price**.
- Responsibility 6: declaring every requirement high priority is **neither realistic nor collaborative**.
- Pick the **decision makers** and a **decision rule** before the first significant decision arrives.
- The **requirements baseline** matters more than the sign-off ritual; a signature only helps when its meaning is explicitly shared.
- Never use sign-off as a weapon to **freeze** requirements or to assign blame.
- If agreement is missing, log it on the **risk list** and proceed cautiously rather than treating silence as consent.
- Agile does not sign off the whole scope, but it **still needs a vision** and it still freezes scope **within** an iteration.

## Summary

- Good requirements come from a **partnership**, not a one-way handover.
- Identify the full stakeholder set — use an **organization chart** so no affected department is missed.
- Make two-way expectations explicit through the **Bill of Rights** and **Bill of Responsibilities**.
- Resistance to a requirements process usually comes from **fear, uncertainty, or a bad past experience** — treat it with evidence of past pain and with early developer and tester involvement.
- The **baseline** is the reference point against which change is measured; sign-off only has value when everyone shares its meaning.
- When approval is missing, **manage it as a risk** rather than pretending it is not.
