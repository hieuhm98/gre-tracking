# Team Management & Communication

## 1. What actually decides a team's effectiveness

> **The strongest predictor of team effectiveness is not the individual ability of its members, but how safe it feels to speak honestly.**

**A team of strong people where nobody dares say *"I do not understand"* or *"I think this approach is wrong"* performs worse than an average team where people speak plainly.** The reason is simple: **bad information is withheld until it is too late.**

**Four signs a team does NOT have that safety:**

- **Nobody asks questions in meetings**, but everyone discusses afterwards in private.
- **Mistakes are hidden** until they surface on their own.
- **Estimates are inflated** to avoid being questioned when late.
- **Nobody contradicts the PM**, even when the PM is clearly wrong.

**Three things a PM can do to change this:**

**1. Admit your own mistakes first, out loud.** *"I estimated this part wrong, I did not account for data migration."* **Nothing is cheaper for giving others permission to do the same.**

**2. Respond to bad news with questions, not emotion.** *"Thank you for telling me early. What options do we still have?"*

**3. Shield whoever raised the problem from outside pressure.** If a risk-raiser gets blamed once in front of the customer, the team will remember it for a very long time.

## 2. Building a team and allocating work

**Three common allocation mistakes:**

**1. Always giving hard work to the strongest person.** Efficient short term, but long term it creates **a bottleneck and a team that never grows**. Your best person burns out and everyone else learns nothing.

**2. Splitting work so nobody depends on anybody.** It sounds sensible but creates **knowledge islands** — each part understood by exactly one person. **This is the largest people-risk source in software projects.**

**3. Allocating by percentage of time.** *"You are 50 percent on project A and 50 percent on B."* **Context switching has a real cost, and a person split in half usually produces less than 70 percent of what they would produce full-time on one thing.**

**Three replacement principles:**

| Principle | How to do it |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Deliberate pairing** | An experienced person with a learner on important parts, to shrink knowledge islands |
| **Limit parallel work** | One primary task per person; work waiting beats a person waiting between tasks |
| **Assign outcomes, not steps** | State what must be achieved and why, and let the doer choose the method |

**On the difference between a group and a team:** a group becomes a team when they share **a goal whose success is measured by the whole group's result**. If each person is judged only on their own slice, you have individuals working side by side, and **no reason for them to help each other when somebody falls behind.**

## 3. The communication plan

**Most project failures get described as "a communication problem", but that diagnosis is too vague to act on.** More precisely: **the right information does not reach the right people while they can still do something about it.**

**A communication plan answers five questions per audience:**

| Question | Example |
|----------|---------|
| **Who needs to know** | Sponsor, customer, team, dependent teams, operations |
| **What they need** | Sponsors need risks and decisions; the team needs priorities and blockers |
| **At what detail** | Sponsors need one page; the team needs the full picture |
| **How often** | Team daily; sponsor fortnightly; customer at each milestone |
| **Through which channel** | Decisions always in writing; discussion in person |

**Four practical principles:**

**1. Decisions must be written down, even when agreed verbally.** Not from distrust, but because **three months later nobody remembers exactly what was agreed.** One recorded line saves hours of argument.

**2. Bad news goes in person, not by email.** Email for bad news creates misreadings and defensiveness. **Call first, then send written confirmation.**

**3. Whoever is affected must hear it from you first, not from someone else.** This is the single most important trust rule in a project.

**4. Do not report progress alone.** Every report should carry: **where we are, where the trend is heading, which risks are growing, and what you need from the reader.** A report without that last part leads to no action.

## 4. Honest status reporting

**Three reporting failures PMs commonly commit:**

**1. Green until red.** A project is reported *"on track"* for ten weeks, then in week eleven becomes *"two months late"*. **No project truly goes from green to red in a week — it was amber for a long time and nobody dared say so.**

**2. Reporting activity only.** *"This week the team met operations, finished the design document, and closed 14 tickets."* **That is a list of work done, not a status.** The real question is: **how much closer to the finish are we?**

**3. Hiding risks to protect appearances.** An unspoken risk does not disappear; it only loses its chance of early treatment.

**The structure of a good status report — four parts, one page:**

> **1. Overall status** — green, amber, red, with **one sentence of why**.
> **2. Progress against plan** — based on accepted work, plus an updated forecast.
> **3. Open risks and issues** — only those the reader needs, with the action underway.
> **4. Decisions or support I need from you** — specific, with dates.

**On using amber properly:** **amber means *"I can handle this but you should know"*.** A PM who never reports amber has removed their own warning instrument. **A project that goes amber early and returns to green is a sign of good management, not weakness.**

## 5. Meetings: fewer but more useful

**Every meeting is an expense: six people for an hour costs six person-hours.** The question is not *"should we meet"* but **"does this meeting create more value than six person-hours?"**

**Four rules for useful meetings:**

**1. Every meeting needs a purpose of one of three kinds:** **deciding** something, **solving** something, or **aligning** understanding of something. **A meeting to "share updates" should be replaced by a document.**

**2. Anyone not needed for that purpose should not be invited.** And invitees must be free to say *"I do not need to be in this"* without being seen as uncooperative.

**3. End with three recorded lines:** what was decided, who does what, by when. **A meeting without those three lines will be held again in two weeks.**

**4. If only two people genuinely need to discuss it, do not invite four others to listen.**

**On daily and status meetings:** a short alignment session has real value, but **it must be about impediments and flow of work, not each person reporting to the PM.** Once a daily turns into reporting, the team optimises for sounding good rather than for delivering.

## 6. Conflict and difficult conversations

**Conflict is NOT a sign of a troubled team — a team that never conflicts has usually stopped speaking honestly.**

**Distinguish two kinds:**

| | Task conflict | Relationship conflict |
|---|--------------|----------------------|
| **About** | Approach, priorities, design | Respect, power, feeling unfairly treated |
| **Value** | **Beneficial** — it produces better decisions | **Harmful** — it destroys collaboration |
| **Handling** | Bring it into the open, decide on criteria | Handle privately, quickly, never let it accumulate |

> **The most common PM mistake: letting relationship conflict persist in the hope it resolves itself.** It does not; it mutates into fake task conflict, where two people oppose every proposal the other makes for reasons entirely different from the ones they state.

**Four steps for a difficult conversation:**

**1. In private, as early as possible.** Do not save it for the quarterly review.

**2. Start with a specific observation, not a character judgement.** *"In the last three meetings, when Minh made a proposal you objected before hearing it out"* — not *"you are difficult to work with"*.

**3. Ask before concluding.** Very often there is a reason you do not know.

**4. End with one concrete change both sides agree to**, and a date to review it.

**When the team is overloaded — this is a PM's problem to resolve rather than the team's to endure:**

- **Do not say *"just push a bit harder"*.** That only converts your problem into theirs.
- **Make a real decision:** cut scope, move the date, or add someone who actually helps. **If you can do none of those three, say so plainly and own the consequence** — teams can handle a hard truth, but not the feeling of being abandoned.

## 7. Key takeaways

- **The strongest predictor of team effectiveness is safety to speak honestly**, not individual ability.
- Signs of low safety: **no questions in meetings, hidden mistakes, inflated estimates, nobody contradicting the PM**.
- **A PM admitting their own mistakes first** is the cheapest way to permit others to do so.

- **Splitting work so nobody depends on anybody creates knowledge islands** — the largest people risk.

- The precise communication problem is **the right information not reaching the right people while they can act**.
- **Decisions must be written down**, since three months later nobody remembers what was agreed.
- **Bad news goes in person, not by email** — call first, confirm in writing after.
- **Whoever is affected must hear it from you first, not from someone else.**
- Every report needs **where we are, the trend, growing risks, and what you need from the reader**.
- **No project goes from green to red in a week** — it was amber for a long time.

- **Six people for an hour costs six person-hours** — meetings must decide, solve, or align.
- **A meeting to share updates should be replaced by a document.**
- **A meeting not ending in decision, owner, deadline will be held again.**
- **A team that never conflicts has usually stopped speaking honestly.**

- Difficult conversations: **specific observations rather than character judgements, ask before concluding**.
- When the team is overloaded, the PM must **cut scope, move the date, or add help** — not ask for more effort.

## 8. Summary

- **Safety to speak honestly is the foundation**, and every other team technique only works once it exists.
- **Work allocation must account for knowledge islands and context-switching cost**, not only short-term efficiency.
- **Honest reporting means using amber at the right time** and always stating what you need from the reader.
- **Task conflict belongs in the open; relationship conflict must be handled privately and fast.**
